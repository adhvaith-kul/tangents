# @TimSweeneyEpic: how it writes
- Volume and mix: high, several posts a day, many of them replies. Originals on court rulings, app-store policy, Epic and Unreal news; long reply chains arguing with developers, lawyers and critics; quote posts of Apple, Google and Valve news with a verdict.
- Length and shape: one to four plain sentences, 80 to 280 characters; a ruling day brings a run of posts. Replies are often as long as the original.
- Mechanics: full sentences, standard punctuation, an engineer's precise wording ("monopoly gatekeeper", "anti-steering", "competing payment methods"). Names judges and courts. Occasional exclamation mark on a win. Almost no emoji or hashtags. Links to filings and rulings.
- Openers and closers: opens with the verdict ("Apple's delaying tactics have failed", "This is a big win for developers"). Closes on a principle (choice, competition, fair fees) or a pointed question to Apple.
- Subjects: Epic v. Apple and Epic v. Google, app-store fees worldwide, the Epic Games Store, Fortnite on mobile, Unreal Engine, AI in game development, Steam policies, regulation in the EU, UK, Japan and Australia.
- Register: earnest, combative, certain; a programmer arguing in public. Praises Apple or Google once in a while when they do something he likes.
- Never: jokes at fans, crude language, hedging, emoji strings.
- In the style of:
  1. Another court has found that a store monopoly cannot set its own rules for competitors. Developers everywhere benefit.
  2. If a commission can't be justified by real costs, it isn't a fee. It's a tax on competition.
  3. Unreal Engine 5 developers shipped more games this quarter than in all of 2023. Thank you to every team building with it.
